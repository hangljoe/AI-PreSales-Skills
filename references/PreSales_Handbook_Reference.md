## 1. Define PreSales and Your Importance in the SaaS Business Model

**Definition.** PreSales covers the space between a prospect's first interest and the commitment to buy: needs assessment, qualification, demonstrations, technical consultation, tailored solution design and sometimes a PoC.

**What PreSales does for the client:** figure out what they need, qualify whether the solution fits, show the ropes (demo), give expert technical advice, tailor the experience (sometimes a mini version of the solution).

**SaaS implications:** continuous client engagement (renewals), immediate feedback loops, a value-driven model (value must be delivered every subscription period).

### 1.1 Challenges of PreSales

Twelve named challenges: understanding client needs (including what is not said); complex products and services; customisation vs. configuration; Sales vs. PreSales alignment; **"your client is your reseller"** (the client must sell internally to its decision board); coordination across product, sales, marketing and service; constant learning; very long (B2B) sales cycles; competitive marketplace; changing market trends; unpredictability; maintaining motivation.

### 1.2 Why PreSales Exists

Understanding client needs; enhancing client understanding of complex products; risk mitigation (fewer post-sale conflicts); effective communication (bridge from jargon to client language); increased sales success rate; understanding the market (first to see shifts); product improvement through field feedback.

### 1.3 How PreSales Makes a Difference

- Consultative, not aggressive: aim for the decision that genuinely benefits the client.
- Contributions: client satisfaction, successful sales, influence on product development.
- **Nine key-takeaway roles:** Discovery; Develop a Champion; Enable your Champion; Deliver a Great Demo (a story with the product as hero, not every feature); Deliver a Great Story; Trusted Advisor; Team Sports; Prove Your Value (including against the **status quo**, not only competitors); Learn Your Lessons.

### 1.4 So What

- Value to the organisation: strategic positioning, product relevance, risk management (first line of defence against post-sale conflict).
- Value to the client: guided decision making, future-proof solutions, assured satisfaction, raising industry standards, early reading of market trends.

## 2. The Role of a PreSales Professional

### 2.1 What Is a PreSales Professional?

The person at the centre of giving prospects the details, demonstrations and reassurance they need to buy (illustrated with a drone-sale story: listen, demo, hands-on trial, support promise).

### 2.2 Key Responsibilities

Seven: **Requirement analysis** (functional and technical discovery, documented in the **Opportunity Scoping Document, OSD**); **Qualify** (can the goals be achieved with our solution, and is the client willing to invest); **Proposal development** with Sales; **Solution design**; **Client education**; **Demonstrations**; **Liaison with product teams**.

### 2.3 Required Skills and Competencies

Functional and technical expertise; excellent communication (written and spoken); problem solving; relationship building (internal and external); understanding of the sales process.

### 2.4 The Value of a PreSales Professional

Reducing sales cycles; increasing sales conversions (fit drives win rate); enhancing client satisfaction; feedback loop for product teams.

### 2.5 Challenges in PreSales

Keeping up with product changes; managing client expectations (do not "promise the moon"); navigating internal dynamics.

### 2.6 Evolving Role in the Digital Age

- Shift to virtual demos, AI-driven insight, CRM as a holistic client picture; keep the human element.
- Boundaries between sales, PreSales and post-sales blur: PreSales are "promise-makers and promise-keepers".
- **70/30 rule:** spend 70% of your time on deals and 30% on learning and upskilling (repeated in 3.8 and 18.4 as "no more than 70% on deals").

### 2.7 The PreSales Mindset

#### 2.7.1 What Is the PreSales Mindset?

Technical precision plus client-centric empathy. Shift from "let me tell you why we are best" to "help me understand your challenges".
- **Five pillars:** client-centric empathy (the "why" behind the ask); solution-oriented thinking; technical curiosity; collaborative spirit; ethical integrity.
- **Nurturing it:** continuous learning, active listening, "feedback is gold".
- **In action:** customer understanding via discovery, tailored demos, proactive problem solving, long-term relationships, seamless team collaboration.

#### 2.7.2 Challenges to the PreSales Mindset

Sales pressure (short-term targets vs. long-term value); information overload; organisational silos.

#### 2.7.3 Overcoming These Challenges

Balanced goal setting (satisfaction and quality next to targets); prioritisation and time management; internal collaboration platforms.

### 2.8 Ethics in PreSales

Honesty and client benefit even when it delays a sale. Three field dilemmas: the **oversell dilemma** (tell the truth about a missing feature; the client returned later); **unforeseen discovery** (disclose a security gap you stumble on); **commission vs. honesty** (recommend the cheaper edition that fits; trust brings referrals).

### 2.9 Client-Centric Approach

Understand drives, worries, goals and limits, not just features. Learning *why* they need something reveals the real business problem.

### 2.10 Handling Difficult Conversations

Eleven rules: preparation (facts, role-play); right setting (private, face-to-face if possible); listen actively; do not interrupt; stay calm and professional; use "I" statements; seek solutions, not wins; know when to pause; follow up in writing (points, decisions, next steps); reflect and learn; seek feedback.

### 2.11 Building Relationships with Clients

- Value of relationships: trust, client retention, feedback loop, referrals.
- Practices: the art of listening (empathy, open questions, paraphrase); consistent communication (check-ins, tailored content, celebrate milestones); going beyond business (personalisation, remember details, client events); handling concerns (swift response, transparency, go the extra mile); upselling and cross-selling (evolving needs, relevant offers, exclusive offers); collaborative approach (co-creation, involve key clients in annual or quarterly strategic planning, co-authored case studies); training and education (workshops, online resource hub, feedback on training).

### 2.12 Cross-Functional Collaboration

PreSales interplay with Marketing (field feedback on campaigns), Client Support (recurring issues, consistent promises), Product Management and Development (market needs, gaps), and Professional Services (smooth handover, delivery of what was sold). Consistent communication channels are imperative. Author's practice: regular **moderated cadence meetings** with all departments, agenda collected in advance, minutes with actions, owners and deadlines.

## 3. The Buying / Selling Journey

Core lesson: you do not know the client's buying process and the client does not know your selling process. Deals are lost when decision makers and departments (Purchasing, Legal, IT, Security) are unknown.

### 3.1 The Buying Journey

Eight stages (road-trip metaphor):

| Stage | Client | Seller |
|---|---|---|
| Awareness | Recognises a need, cannot yet articulate it; initial research | Creates awareness (marketing, outreach) |
| Consideration | Researches options; engages content; free trial or pilot | Positions as a potential solution (content, webinars, demos) |
| Decision | Further investigation (integration, security, scalability); vendor comparison; engages Sales | Differentiates; handles concerns and objections |
| Purchase | Completes the transaction | Smooth transition from prospect to client |
| Implementation | Onboarding, data, IT coordination, testing | Integration, migration, configuration, training, support |
| Post-purchase | Adoption, support, feedback, expansion, advocacy | Support, feedback, nurture upsell and referrals |
| Retention & Renewal | Periodic check-ins, renewal discussions | — |
| Re-evaluation | Needs change; explores the market | — |

The journey is non-linear; clients look for value, understanding and trust.

### 3.2 The Different Client Personas and What You Could Do in the Buying Process

**Decision-board departments** (engage early; late involvement delays or kills deals):
- **Finance** — "the logical powerhouse": budget, ROI, savings, vendor financial stability.
- **Legal** — "the bastion of compliance": involve early to avoid last-minute contract problems.
- **IT Security** — "the digital sentinel": show credentials, certifications and compliance from the start.
- **IT** — "the integrators": integration, APIs, integration case studies.
- **Operations** — "the pulse of daily function": real-world demos, pilots, hands-on workshops.
- **Purchasing** — "the grand negotiators": transparent pricing models, flexible terms.

**Six client-intention personas:**
- **Member** — grounds the discussion in daily users' needs; wants evidence (demos, case studies, testimonials).
- **Sponsor** — visionary; wants long-term transformation, scalability, long-term ROI; use storytelling.
- **Champion** — believes in you; do not sell to them, **arm them** (tools, data, narratives, workshops).
- **Detractor** — sceptic; engage, find the root of the reservation, answer with evidence; can become a vocal advocate.
- **Gatekeeper** — procedural guardian; documentation, compliance, standards; sign-off is often the final step.
- **Advisor** — often external consultant; case studies, white papers, benchmarks, future-proofing.

Lesson from the field: build **client maps** (attendees, roles, sentiment, concerns) before key meetings.

### 3.3 The Selling Journey

Each phase has a description, key activities and PreSales actions. Approach flexibly.

#### 3.3.1 All Core Phases

1. **Prospecting / Lead Generation** — PreSales gives technical insight for campaigns and target industries.
2. **Initial Contact** — prep Sales with technical collateral; join if technical questions arise.
3. **Sales Discovery / Sales Qualification** — fit on needs, budget, authority, timeline; "use the BANT (in chapter 5)". PreSales attends and categorises leads by functional and technical fit.
4. **Functional & Technical Discovery** — tech landscape, integration, pain, desired future state, **critical dates and events**. PreSales leads or co-leads and documents.
5. **Product Demonstration & Workshops** — tailored demos, objections, USPs.
6. **Ballpark Pricing** — PreSales flags what drives price (e.g. custom development).
7. **Service Discovery** — mainly Professional Services, to create a **statement of work (SOW)**; may be skipped for small no-config SaaS. PreSales hands over and aligns first.
8. **Proposal & Value Proposition** — PreSales drafts functional and technical sections and validates the feasibility of every promise.
9. **Negotiation** — clarify technical aspects; help find compromises.
10. **Closing** — contract signed; PreSales hands over to Professional Services, Onboarding and Client Success.
11. **Onboarding** — support the implementation team through technical hiccups.

#### 3.3.2 Additional Phases

Expansion / Upselling (post-sale); Renewal & Advocacy (case studies); **RFX process** (can start at any point; PreSales leads responses); Long-term Development (nurture not-yet-ready leads); **Disqualification** (confirm the misfit, communicate clearly, refer elsewhere if possible); Win/Lost Analysis.

#### 3.3.3 Best Practices

Regularly review sales stages; leverage CRM; align Sales and Marketing (messaging, qualification criteria, handovers); continuous training; feedback loop.

### 3.4 The Differences of Both Journeys and How We Overcome It

- **Bridging the gap:** early engagement (share your selling journey early); stakeholder mapping (advise the client which departments to involve); guide the client (you sell daily, they buy every few years); **the client is the reseller**; flexibility (non-linear journeys); mutual value proposition (win-win).
- **Visualise the journey** as a roadmap of stages, activities and stakeholders and present it early. Benefits: aligned expectations, efficient resource allocation, trust, anticipating challenges, collaboration. Author's practice: discuss the buying journey in every discovery session.

### 3.5 The Role of PreSales in the Buying/Selling Process

Requirement gathering; demonstrations; solution crafting; objection handling; collaboration; feedback loop; technical expertise; training and education; risk mitigation.

### 3.6 Strategies for Effective Selling

Eleven: be prepared; active listening; personalisation; building trust (realistic expectations, expertise); leveraging testimonials (social proof); objection handling as opportunity; understanding the client journey; continuous learning; use of technology; long-term relationships; empathy.

### 3.7 Challenges in the Buying/Selling Process

Long sales cycles; multiple stakeholders with conflicting priorities (e.g. IT vs. CFO vs. end users); competition (win on pain solved, not feature count); changing needs.

### 3.8 Overcoming Challenges

Stakeholder mapping; value-proposition clarity; flexibility; consistent value-adding follow-up; feedback mechanism; cross-functional collaboration; continual learning ("dedicate 30% of your time to learning"); use of technology (CRM, AI insight, demo automation, click-through demos, canned videos in client portals).

### 3.9 The Evolution of the Buying/Selling Process

Informed decision making (clients arrive pre-researched; seller validates and clarifies); digital touchpoints; data-driven selling. Keep the personal touch: a phone call still builds trust.

### 3.10 When to Engage with the Clients from a Sales/PreSales Perspective

**Engage early**, ideally before the client is aware of the need. Why: thought leadership, relationships, shaping perceptions. Channels: conferences and trade shows, blogs, LinkedIn and networks, webinars. Early engagement is not only Marketing's job.

### 3.11 Selling as One

- Selling is a team sport. "Selling as One" = one consistent story from Marketing, PreSales and Sales, efficiency, no client repeating themselves, broader solutions.
- The team: Marketing, PreSales (interpreters), Sales, Professional Services (tailors), Product Management / Development / Engineering, Client Support, Finance, Legal, Operations.
- **Alignment tools:** handover calls (relay-race baton); **unified documentation (the OSD)**; CRM as the shared data pool; regular sync meetings (weekly or bi-weekly); unified communication platforms; joint training; clear handoff protocols (checklists, templates, CRM notes); feedback mechanisms; integrated tools; celebrate success together; clarify roles; client engagement reviews (no blame).

### 3.12 Best Practices for Selling Journey

Consultative approach ("do not be shy to disqualify"); regular training; CRM and automation; tailored demos; transparent communication, especially when disqualifying; Sales-PreSales collaboration; feedback after each stage; long-term relationships; value proposition tied to business and technical needs; educational content; stay adaptable; robust post-sale support; clear concise proposals; empathy and listening; ethical selling (no vapourware); set clear expectations.

## 4. Developing a Champion

**Definition.** A champion is your advocate inside the client company: they believe in the solution beyond its technical details and understand how it addresses their organisation's needs. For PreSales a champion smooths interactions, explains internal dynamics and speeds approvals.

**Identifying.** Not always the loudest person. Look for pointed questions, keen interest in how the solution works, a clear view of fit with company goals, and the capability, directly or indirectly, to influence the decision.

**Cultivating.**
- Enable the champion to carry the torch: tailored materials, videos, access to specialised portals.
- Build the relationship: trust, mutual respect, a shared success story; position the solution as a piece of the larger organisational puzzle.
- Continuity: regular touchpoints (updates, check-ins, industry insights).
- The champion handles internal obstacles and keeps momentum.
- Appreciate the champion's advocacy (thanks, a note, acknowledgement).

**Field story.** A strong champion separated supporters from detractors, defended the solution and secured an exclusive C-level presentation that won the deal.

## 5. Sales Discovery

Sales Discovery is the first step of a potential relationship: an investigation of the client's situation, problems, goals and needs, usually run by Sales.

**The BANT framework** (the V78 qualification framework):
- **Budget** — financial capability and willingness to invest; avoid proposing beyond reach.
- **Authority** — engage those who can say "yes"; the right party, not just an interested one.
- **Needs** — the challenges and pain, including what is read between the lines.
- **Timeline** — when the client intends to decide or implement (product launch, fiscal year); drives prioritisation and follow-up.

**Documentation: the OSD and CRM.** The OSD captures Sales and PreSales discovery: key challenges and pain, budget, decision-making stakeholders and implementation timelines. Integrate it with CRM; conversation-intelligence tools (e.g. Gong.io) add summaries and insight.

**Why Sales Discovery matters to PreSales:** continuity in conversation (the client never repeats themselves); awareness of promises and commitments made by Sales; addressing client queries proactively.

**When PreSales steps in.** Ideally PreSales engages *after* Sales Discovery, fully briefed on discussions, promises and the BANT result. PreSales joins Sales Discovery when Sales is new or technical nuance is needed.

**Qualify hard at the beginning.** In B2B, second place equals a lost opportunity. Cycles run for months and consume hundreds of hours.

**Cost of not qualifying early (V78 worked example, 8-month cycle):**

| Role | Hours | Rate | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sales | 100 | $50/h | $5,000 |
| PreSales | 70 | $60/h | $4,200 |
| Product Management | 10 | $70/h | $700 |
| **Total** | | | **$9,900** |

Formula per role: Hours × Rate = Cost. The real cost is much higher once you add opportunity cost, resource drain, dilution of focus, financial implications (travel, demos, pilots), overheads, training, tools, travel and accommodation, collateral development and miscellaneous costs (legal, third-party evaluations, entertainment).

**Capacity math (V78):** a PreSales consultant with a **$1.5M quota** and a strong **35% close rate** (roughly every third deal), spending **80–160 hours** per ~$300k deal, with about **1,600 hours** a year, can work **10–20 opportunities**: a **$3–6M pipeline** and about **$1–2M** won. Every unwinnable deal you engage in lowers your chance of making quota.

## 6. Qualify Early, Qualify Hard

Qualifying hard is **strategy, not scepticism**: making sure both vendor and client are on a path to mutual success and a long-term win-win partnership. It is about the client's readiness and the vendor's ability to deliver.

**Four elements (key takeaways):**
1. **Understanding client readiness** — use BANT to gauge readiness to buy.
2. **Assessing fit** — not every prospect fits; assess early whether the solution truly addresses the pain ("like a key for a specific lock").
3. **Stakeholder engagement / engaging decision makers** — clarifies the decision process and exposes bottlenecks early.
4. **Setting clear expectations** — timelines, deliverables, costs, support structures, with no surprises later.

**Author's stance.** Saying "no" to a deal is not bad. Be upfront from the first meeting; if you are not the best fit, say so and suggest other vendors. Always add value, or step back.

## 7. Functional & Technical Discovery

Discovery decides the sale. Based heavily on Peter Cohan's *Doing Discovery*, which V78 recommends reading. Two competitive advantages: client trust grows, and the demo becomes personalised, outcome-oriented and valuable.

**Definition:** discovery is "the process of gathering knowledge to enable a detailed suggestion of a solution to a prospect problem." Skipping it and demoing is like a doctor prescribing without diagnosis. You know you have learned *enough* when you can recommend a solution with confidence.

### 7.1 The Dual Benefits of Discovery

For vendors: understand needs well enough to propose an exact answer. For prospects: see which vendor truly understands them.

### 7.2 The Process of Doing Discovery

Nine steps (objective → activities):
1. **Preparation** — research client, competitors, market, past interactions; prepare open-ended questions.
2. **Initiate engagement** — schedule a dedicated session with key stakeholders; **start with 25 minutes** and ask for more sessions at the end; state the purpose.
3. **Information gathering** — open questions, active listening, write everything down; use recordings or transcripts and AI summaries when allowed.
4. **Technical assessment** (if applicable) — current tools, constraints, rules, integration points.
5. **Stakeholder interviews** — multiple departments; adapt questions per role; ensure confidentiality.
6. **Gap analysis** — current vs. desired state; prioritise gaps by impact and urgency.
7. **Feedback and validation** — present a summary of findings; refine with the client's feedback.
8. **Documentation and sharing** — structured findings in the **OSD** (ch. 8), shared with sales, technical and product teams.
9. **Plan next steps** — tailor the solution or proposal; schedule follow-ups or demos.

### 7.3 Structured Approach to Discovery

Eight stages (after Cohan): Opening; Demographics; Supply Chain & Business Operations; Workflows/Processes (tech landscape); Major Pain; Extended Environment; Culture; Vision Wrap-up.
- Opening: build rapport and **be yourself** (use the LinkedIn profile for genuine topics).
- Supply chain map reveals dependencies, bottlenecks, optimisation opportunities, global risk, and enables holistic solutions.
- **Value Realisation Event (VRE):** a specific, measurable milestone after implementation when the client first sees tangible benefit (efficiency, cost, etc.). Concrete proof of value, the client's "aha" moment.
- Close by connecting identified problems to critical business implications and a shared roadmap.

### 7.4 Detailed Stages and Questions

Each stage has an importance, a reason and typical questions. Representative questions:

#### 7.4.1 Opening: Setting the Stage

Rapport breaks the ice and builds trust. "Tell me – before we dive in – how did you ascend to this position… at [company]?"; "What drove you to your current role…?"; what a typical day looks like; milestones they are proud of; mentors.

#### 7.4.2 Demographics: Getting to the Core

Structural backbone and workflow dynamics; guides customisation and gives context to pain. Team sizes and locations; primary daily workflows; how the structure evolved; key stakeholders per department; the **decision-making process**; remote or hybrid teams; peak seasons; central vs. team-specific tools.

#### 7.4.3 Supply Chain & Business Operations: The Flow of Value

How value is created and delivered. Product journey from idea to delivery; manufacturing or development challenges; inventory and warehousing inefficiencies; distribution channels; returns and defects; operations systems; seasonal peaks; external factors; **operational KPIs**; disruption and contingency handling.

#### 7.4.4 Workflows/Processes Environment: Assessing the Tech Landscape

Compatibility and integration risk; minimal disruption. "Can you walk me through your current software infrastructure?"; future plans; happy with the setup, and if not, the driving need; **experiences in previous software purchases**; competitive solutions in use; recurring tech challenges; consensus on tools; downtime handling; onboarding and training.

#### 7.4.5 Major Pain: Identifying Pain Points and Opportunities

Pain drives tangible value (boat-and-lake analogy: need turns a luxury into a necessity). "In your current processes, what challenges do you face consistently?"; "If you could redesign… what would it be?"; financial or operational impact; pains that became "the norm"; what was tried; morale impact; the one pain to eliminate instantly. Also establish **what the problem is worth** and whether there is a **deadline** to fix it.

#### 7.4.6 Extended Environment: Broadening the Scope

Primary pain has a domino effect (kitchen-oven analogy). Related areas feeling strain; impact on larger business objectives; bottlenecked processes; stakeholders outside the department affected.

#### 7.4.7 Culture: Navigating Company Dynamics

Culture decides adoption. How they approach new software adoptions; predefined transition methods; stakeholders in adoption decisions; adoption stories (good and bad); keeping teams aligned; non-negotiable values; handling resistance.

#### 7.4.8 Vision Wrap-up: Clarifying the Way Forward

Not a recap but confirmation of understanding plus a forward-looking plan. "Considering the challenges we discussed, what do you foresee if no changes are made?" (cost of inaction); "What events or triggers will signify that the proposed solution is delivering its value?"; "Shall we outline the next steps…?"

### 7.5 Techniques for Effective Discovery

- **Open-ended questions** ("Can you describe the challenges…" instead of "Do you face challenges…").
- **Comparative questions** (this year vs. last year, vs. initial expectations).
- **SPIN** — Situation, Problem, Implication, Need-Payoff.
- Uncover **critical pain points** ("what keeps them up at night") and their broader impact.
- **Strategic silence** after a question; **active listening** with summaries ("So, if I understand correctly…").
- **Empathy mapping** — Says, Feels, Does, Thinks.
- **SWOT analysis** of the prospect.
- **The 5 Whys** — repeat "why" to reach the root cause.

### 7.6 Common Challenges in Discovery

Surface-level information; defensiveness (fear of being "sold to"); misalignment between stakeholders; time constraints.

### 7.7 Overcoming Discovery Challenges

Build trust (intent is value, not a pitch); engage all stakeholders; flexible approach (shorter sessions, change direction); leverage testimonials that show the value of thorough discovery.

### 7.8 Translating Discovery into Value Propositions

Align each solution element to a stated pain; showcase an **ROI** model built from discovery insights; craft tailored demos that simulate the discussed challenges.

### 7.9 Doing a Demo in a Discovery

"Never ever do a Demo without Discovery (Never?)". When Sales promised a demo or the client insists (V78 points to a **Give-Get** approach):
- Start with a crisp pitch and broad visual vision, **5–10 minutes** maximum.
- Then a short **Look & Feel demo** (trailer, not movie): navigation, dashboards, a simple use case.
- Avoid the rabbit hole; acknowledge deep questions and defer them to after discovery.
- Run **discovery inside the demo** by interspersing probing questions (for experienced PreSales only).
- Offer supplementary material (brochures, white papers, automated demos) if they want more.

### 7.10 How to Learn Doing Discovery

Read *Doing Discovery* (Cohan). Practise mock sessions with colleagues or sales partners; let conversation flow; listen actively (Rosenberg recommended); get feedback after each mock; keep refining.

## 8. The Discovery Summary / Opportunity Scoping Document (OSD)

The OSD captures the essence of discovery and the **mutual understanding** reached. For the seller it is the roadmap for pitches and demos; for the client it proves they were understood. Format is free (document, PowerPoint or CRM summary) as long as it accurately reflects what was discussed and agreed. It can be client-facing, and it also serves Professional Services (services estimate), Product Management (gaps), leadership reviews and the implementation handover.

### 8.1 The Importance of an OSD

A bridge between discovery and the rest of the journey: a **testament to understanding**, a **foundation for future engagements** (every demo and proposal references it) and **internal consistency** (a single source of truth for the team).

### 8.2 Structuring the OSD

The V78 structure (extend if the business needs it):
1. **Document History** — versions, modification dates, contributors.
2. **Executive Summary** — company snapshot, principal challenges, how the offering helps.
3. **Company Profile** — size, industry, current solutions, key stakeholders, organisational structure, market standing, ethos.
4. **Goals, Challenges and Major Pain Points** — issues, implications, probable root causes.
5. **Desired Outcomes and Vision** — objectives, what success looks like, broader business goals.
6. **Supply Chain Map** — how products or services flow; bottlenecks and crucial nodes.
7. **IT Overview and Architecture** — systems, interfaces, legacy systems.
8. **Project Prioritisation and Business Releases** — the order in which the client wants to roll out or address challenges.
9. **Assumptions & Gaps** — shared assumptions so client, Sales and Professional Services understand the same thing.
10. **Modules & Detailed Scoping** — solution modules and functionality mapped to needs.
11. **Meeting Notes** — discussions and decisions, structured around action items, owners and follow-ups.

### 8.3 Tips for Crafting an Impactful OSD

**Reflect, do not regurgitate** (e.g. "We are struggling with timely deliveries" becomes a statement of delivery-process challenges causing revenue loss and lower satisfaction); use visual aids (flowcharts, tables); clarity is king; **validate with the prospect** (at least the crucial elements) before further steps.

### 8.4 Using the OSD Effectively

Review it before every engagement; use it to drive internal decisions (customisations, feature priorities, demo focus); use it as a feedback mechanism (invite the prospect to review it regularly; their changes show shifting priorities).

### 8.5 Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

Being overly generic (tailor it); overloading with information (litmus test: does it add value to understanding? otherwise appendix); not keeping it updated (living document, review before key touchpoints).

## 9. Technical & Functional Qualification (TFQ)

TFQ determines whether the solution matches the prospect's technical specifications **and** meets their practical (functional) requirements, like finding the jigsaw piece that fits and completes the picture. V78 describes TFQ qualitatively; it defines no scoring model.

### 9.1 Using the OSD to Qualify from a Functional & Technical Perspective

The OSD acts as a sieve.
- **Functional qualification with the OSD:** use *Goals, Challenges and Major Pain Points* to understand inefficiencies, implications and root causes; map each functional challenge to features or modules; check against *Desired Outcomes and Vision* so the solution also enables aspirations.
- **Technical qualification through the OSD:** use *IT Overview and Architecture* to validate compatibility and seamless integration (disruption only if strategic); use *Assumptions & Gaps* as the touchstone for uncertainty and friction.
- **Confluence:** *Project Prioritisation and Business Releases* informs both functional sequencing and technical readiness for each rollout.

### 9.2 Key Components of Technical & Functional Qualification

| Functional qualification ("what": will it address the business needs and pains?) | Technical qualification ("how": can it be implemented in their environment?) |
|---|---|
| **Understanding business needs** (operational, strategic, industry-specific) | **Understanding technical infrastructure** (software, hardware, data workflows, integration touchpoints, legacy) |
| **Mapping solutions to needs** (a feature or module for every pain) | **Compatibility assessment** (fits without unforeseen disruption) |
| **Assessing value proposition** (efficiency, new revenue, transformation) | **Integration capabilities** (APIs, middleware, connectors; seamless data flow) |
| **Future-proofing** (scalable, adaptable, resilient) | **Technical constraints and assumptions** (legacy, security policies, data storage) |

The two are **two sides of one coin** and **iterative**: a feature that cannot be implemented loses its value, and a technically perfect solution that misses core needs is a square peg in a round hole. Technical findings (e.g. a legacy system) can reveal functional workarounds and vice versa.

### 9.3 Opportunity Review Call (ORC)

A collaborative call to **assess, strategise and qualify** opportunities.
- **Not every opportunity goes to the ORC.** Bring those where technical or functional fit needs discussion, or where about **90% of requirements are met but the missing 10% needs commitments from other departments**.
- **Purpose:** beyond review, a nexus for qualification. Decide whether the opportunity is worth pursuing, identify bottlenecks, set a coherent strategy.
- **Rhythm:** **weekly** cadence recommended.
- **Participants:** Sales (client dynamics), PreSales (technical understanding and positioning), Professional Services (deployment experience), Product Management (capabilities and roadmap), Development (feasibility and customisations).
- **Preparation:** share a clear agenda and the OSD a few days in advance.
- **Tracking and documentation:** record discussions, decisions and action steps; use a dedicated ORC channel (Slack or Microsoft Teams) as the archive.
- **Aftermath:** translate insights into actions (technical deep dives, aligning with Product Management on upcoming features); keep momentum through the channel.

### 9.4 After Technical & Functional Qualification – What Next?

Demo preparation; demonstrations and workshops (interactive co-creation); crafting the proposal (specs, integration, customisations, foreseen challenges); resource estimation (time, people, hardware); implementation roadmap (milestones, timelines, deliverables); closing; PoC where needed; the RFX process; negotiation (collaboration, win-win).

## 10. The Return on Investment (ROI) & Value Calculation

**Why ROI matters.** It turns claims into evidence ("invest X, expect Y, based on these data"), is the yardstick for comparing options, and aligns a buying committee around one metric. It builds credibility, offers a comparative tool and aligns stakeholders.

**The V78 formula:**

> ROI = [(Net Profit − Cost of Investment) / Cost of Investment] × 100%

Net Profit is the money earned from the investment ("take-home"); Cost of Investment is what was originally spent. The hard part is accurate inputs: hidden costs and unexpected gains.

**Cost types (investment):** Direct costs (software, hardware, services); Implementation costs (training, customisation, integration); Maintenance costs; **Opportunity costs** (benefits forgone by choosing one option or changing the current way).

**Why SaaS costs are easier to determine:** predictable subscription pricing; reduced infrastructure and hardware; centralised upgrades and maintenance (bundled); transparent tiered pricing; fewer hidden implementation costs; scalability.

**Net profit (benefit) types:** Direct benefits (revenue up, operating cost down); Operational efficiency; Long-term strategic value (loyalty, data-driven decisions, competitive edge); Intangible benefits (brand perception, employee satisfaction, client experience).

**Why benefits are harder than costs:** variability of outcomes; long-term vs. short-term; intangibles; external factors; dependency on implementation; subjectivity; measurement complexity; delayed realisation.

**Value proposition = the "why" and "how" behind the ROI.** A bare "20% ROI over three years" becomes convincing only when tied to specific pains, streamlined operations, support and integration. Ten elements: address client pain points; balance tangible and intangible benefits; build for tomorrow; stand out from the crowd; proof (success stories, testimonials); less is more; anticipate concerns; know your audience (CTO vs. CFO); smooth transitions (integration); beyond purchase (support, training).

**Tools and techniques:** ROI calculators (V78 names "The ROI Shop"); case studies with achieved ROI; industry benchmarks; validation by consulting firms (e.g. Accenture, KPMG) to make the ROI "bulletproof".

**Pitfalls:** over-promising; overlooking hidden costs (training, downtime, integration complexity); generic one-size-fits-all calculations.

**Addressing the challenges:** consultative approach (deep discovery); **conservative estimates** (underpromise, overdeliver); **post-implementation check** of realised vs. projected ROI.

**Emotional ROI:** trust in the vendor can outweigh favourable numbers; cultural fit; crafting a vision; continuous feedback; storytelling with success stories; addressing emotional concerns (change anxiety); the human element. Takeaways: building trust, understanding corporate culture, crafting a vision.

## 11. The Demo (Tell the Story)

The demo is where the abstract becomes tangible. It should be a conversation, not a lecture, and it builds trust ("seeing is believing"). Structure it as a story: challenge, the product as **hero**, overcoming obstacles, resolution. Key takeaways: visualisation, engagement, trust-building.

### 11.1 The Science behind the Demo

#### 11.1.1 Limbic Persona-Based Selling: Navigating the Emotional Labyrinths of the Client Brain

- The limbic system drives emotion, motivation and decisions; emotion sits at the core of every client decision.
- Combine **personas** (semi-fictional ideal clients built from research, interviews and client-facing teams) with emotionally charged **visuals** and **narratives**.
- **Step-by-step:** craft the personas → speak the limbic language (visual metaphors, e.g. fortress or shield for security, tangled threads straightening for data chaos to clarity) → narrate the tale in three phases (**Introduction to the Challenge → Development of Aspirations → Climax with Product Resolution**) → use limbic triggers (colour: blue = trust and calm, red = urgency; patterns, sound) → practical implementation in tailored demos, adapting live.
- Validate with feedback and train the team in the method.
- **Hiccups:** decisions mix emotion and logic (do not ignore budget and guidelines); avoid over-generalising people into one persona; authenticity is essential (manipulation destroys trust).

#### 11.1.2 Visual Picture-based Selling

"A picture is worth 1000 words". The brain prioritises visual processing; V78 cites images processed **about 60,000 times faster than text** and better retained. Pair visuals with limbic selling genuinely, not manipulatively.

#### 11.1.3 Storytelling: Narrating a Relatable Journey

Tell the *right* story: know the audience via personas, then make the product the **hero** that resolves the persona's challenge. Storytelling humanises and contextualises the product and creates connection. It matters because it creates relatability, enhances retention (stories are remembered better than facts) and drives engagement. Field story: bringing in an "A-Team" consultant with a matching success story beat a better-connected competitor.

#### 11.1.4 Incorporating Emotion into the Demo

Human element (stories of people who benefited); empathy (show you understand their hurdles); enthusiasm (genuine belief is contagious and cannot be faked); build an **emotional arc** from their world, to the product as beacon of change, to impact.

#### 11.1.5 Engagement Strategies

- **Interaction:** ask reflective questions (not tests); seek feedback intermittently ("I pause here for a second, do you have any questions?"); listen actively; watch your pace.
- **Pacing:** fast-paced segments for new sections and highlights; slower detailed explanations for complexity; mindful transitions.
- **Anchoring:** strong visuals, emotive statements, reiteration (loop back to anchors).

#### 11.1.6 Understanding the Psychology of Attention

Engaging start (statistic, question, anecdote); novelty; value proposition early ("What is in it for me?"); comfortable environment; **breaks every 45–60 minutes** (average focused attention span about 45 minutes; announce breaks upfront); interactive elements (polls, Q&A); multi-sensory engagement; cultural and demographic considerations.

### 11.2 Demo Process for PreSales Professionals

Eight steps:
1. **Pre-demo research** — discovery notes, industry and news, attendees and their priorities.
2. **Demo planning** — sections, timing incl. Q&A, backup plans.
3. **Tailoring content** — features that address their pains; industry scenarios; no generic pitch.
4. **Technical setup** — test equipment, connection, backup devices.
5. **Execution** — context and agenda, benefits and value, engagement, answers, summary and next steps.
6. **Addressing questions** — encourage throughout; if unsure, commit to answer after.
7. **Feedback collection** — surveys, 1:1 feedback, analyse trends.
8. **Follow-up** — thank-you, resources and answers, schedule next meetings.

### 11.3 The Structure of a Successful Demo

Four demo types: **Inspiration Look & Feel Session** (teaser, trailer), **Deep Dive Demo** (detailed exploration), **Q&A Session** (audience-driven), **RFX Demo** (structured around the RFP's requirements). Structures vary with the client's use cases.

#### 11.3.1 The Look & Feel Demo

Overall feel and design, not technical depth; a movie trailer. Seven parts:
1. **Introduction** — set the stage; **Picture Pitch**; brief overview incl. value.
2. **Interface and Design** — UI highlights; navigation overview.
3. **Key Features Spotlight** — a few standout features in the context of client value; real-world application.
4. **User Experience** — ease of use; responsiveness across devices.
5. **Customisation and Personalisation** — themes, dashboards, settings.
6. **Feedback and Engagement Tools** — in-product feedback and engagement features.
7. **Conclusion and Next Steps** — reiterate value, explain the next phase, open the floor.

Even here, run a discovery first, even if only **15 minutes**. Combining discovery and Look & Feel is for experienced presenters only.

#### 11.3.2 Deep Dive Demo (The Standard Demo)

Eight-part structure:
1. **Introduction** — team introductions in your own words (name, title, background, why you are the expert, a personal touch); ground rules (when to ask questions, break schedule); **a break every 40–60 minutes**; roadmap of the day.
2. **Picture Pitch** — about **ten minutes** of engaging imagery covering challenges, solution and benefits; if the audience is eager, a brief glimpse of the application (UI, dashboards, analytics). After about 40 minutes of talking, a **5–10 minute break**.
3. **Explaining the Scenes** — if several modules (e.g. Global Trade Management and Transport Management), lay out sequence, timeline, personas and how the processes interrelate.
4. **Setting the Scene 1** — introduce the **2–4 personas**, their challenges and an end-to-end process preview.
5. **Tell-Show-Tell for each persona** — Tell (the persona's struggle and the solution as "magic wand"), Show (clear and simple, not every detail), Tell (reiterate real-world value).
6. **Closing Scene 1** — tangible and intangible organisational benefits, case examples; recommended break after each scene.
   Repeat 4–6 for Scene 2 and further scenes.
7. **Overall Benefits** — the grand narrative; testimonials (ideally video) and case studies.
8. **Closing & Next Steps** — summary, benefits, concrete next steps; address the varied interests of finance, IT, security (split sessions if needed); never start with a boring company overview.

#### 11.3.3 The Q&A Demo

Precision, clarity, immediacy. **Collect questions upfront** (discovery, email or a prep call); never start without them. Prioritise the client's top **3–4 questions** and start with them; then a brief intermission for spontaneous questions. Elements of Deep Dive or Look & Feel can be mixed in. If no questions come in, **call each participant individually**.

#### 11.3.4 RFX Demo

The RFX gives a starting point, not the full picture. Run **discovery sessions** to confirm and deepen it. Approach like a Deep Dive but focused strictly on stated requirements; start with what matters most and **reshape the client's agenda if necessary**. Interleave Q&A. Bring an **A-Team** (Professional Services, Product Management, Client Success). Address unspoken needs. Field story: stepping back from an unresponsive RFP made the client open up, which led to discovery and a win.

### 11.4 The Demo Preparation

Seven steps:
1. **Deep dive into client needs** — revisit discovery notes, industry-specific challenges, prioritise pains.
2. **Storyboard the demo** — plan the flow on the 11.3 structure; select scenarios and personas.
3. **Configure / customise the environment** — client branding, data, configuration; avoid generic setups.
4. **Anticipate questions and concerns** — prepare answers; admitting "I don't know yet" builds trust and earns another meeting.
5. **Dry run** — stable environment; run it **with the Sales Director**; test for glitches; backup plans such as pre-recorded segments.
6. **Rehearse** — multiple times; mock demo in front of colleagues unfamiliar with the client.
7. **Gather supplementary materials** — case studies, white papers, pre-recorded and click-through demos.

### 11.5 The Demonstration

**Setup with Sales.** Sales acts as moderator: manages time, fields questions, weaves in client success stories, reads the room (champions, decision makers, detractors) and keeps energy up.

#### 11.5.1 The Picture Pitch

- Rapidly cycle through images, **each shown for 5–10 seconds**, in sync with your speech. The images are not the focus; they keep attention locked on the speaker.
- Images symbolise key themes (e.g. Rocky Balboa for the champion, the "A-Team" for an expert team, a heavy locker for security, a globe with lines for connectivity).
- Benefits: **engagement**, **memory imprint**, **emotional connection**, **narrative flexibility** (adjust on the fly).
- Requires practice: alignment of images, rhythm and reading reactions. Author's story: a sceptical 30-year veteran called it the best presentation he had ever seen.

#### 11.5.2 Tell-Show-Tell with Personas

Tell-Show-Tell (TST) combined with Limbic Persona-Based Selling:
- **Tell (set the stage):** introduce the persona, their needs, emotional triggers and what they seek. Example: "Meet Alex. Alex is an Achiever…"
- **Show (demonstration):** show the features that solve that persona's challenges.
- **Tell (reiteration):** recap and validate the value in the persona's terms ("Alex is not just reacting to market changes; he is anticipating them").
- Multiple personas: combine in a summary slide or run a process from persona one to persona two (template and video on www.presales-handbook.com).

#### 11.5.3 Engaging the Audience

Interactive elements and hands-on moments; checkpoints; visual aids; live polls; breaks; interactive segments; real-world scenarios; address each stakeholder (CTO on specs, CEO on ROI and scale).

#### 11.5.4 Handling Objections During the Demo

- Objections signal engagement, not roadblocks. **Never take it personally.**
- Five-step handling: listen carefully; stay calm and professional; clarify and educate; provide evidence; follow up (check the client is satisfied).
- **Park** questions that need more thought until a break or post-demo.
- Acknowledge (validate their right to raise it) and pivot to how the solution addresses it; ask probing questions when unclear; anticipate; **admit uncertainty** and commit to answer.
- Field story: interrupting a sceptical attendee backfired. The lesson is "listening, listening, and listening".

### 11.6 After the Demo

**Golden hours.** The first **24–48 hours after the demo** are the "golden hours" (the term comes from emergency medicine). Actions in this window have the most impact:
- **Thank-you note** — personalised, reiterates key points, delivers requested resources.
- **Feedback collection** — short survey; V78 highlights a Sales Director who calls every participant one by one.
- **Internal feedback round** — team debrief (anonymous survey for larger teams).
- **Meeting recap** — content and commitments.
- **Solidify impressions** — reinforce key value points.
- **Address concerns** — give room for doubts raised after reflection.
- **Guide towards a decision** — nudge to the next logical step.

Further post-demo practices:
- **Addressing questions and concerns:** compile and categorise all questions (assign a scribe); research and respond specifically (add a short recording or video if useful); keep channels open.
- **Sharing supplementary materials:** documentation, case studies, videos, **click-through demos**. Less is more: do not send ten items at once; create one insightful touchpoint a week.
- **Scheduling a follow-up meeting:** "The more touchpoints you have, the less they speak with other vendors." Use it to delve deeper, address concerns and plan implementation.
- **Collaborating with Sales:** share insights, craft a joint strategy for hesitant prospects, **stakeholder mapping** to tailor follow-ups.
- **Analysing demo feedback:** identify patterns, refine the demo, train presenters.
- **Staying on the prospect's radar:** regular check-ins, product updates, engagement content.
- **Closing the loop, when to move on:** recognise the signs (repeated delays, generic or no responses), seek feedback on the hesitation, leave the door open.

### 11.7 Why Demos Fail and How to Avoid It

Cost of failure: lost time and resources, damaged reputation, lost revenue (incl. future business), loss of motivation.

Common causes, each with a solution: lack of preparation; not tailored to the audience; overcomplicating; ignoring the business perspective (tie features to business benefit); not addressing questions; technical glitches (backups, offline versions, redundant internet; be transparent); **overselling**; failing to engage all stakeholders; ignoring the competitor landscape; no clear next steps; no storytelling (make the prospect the protagonist); slow follow-up (plan it before the demo); rigid demo tools; poor aesthetics; poor objection handling; lack of interactivity; insufficient rehearsal.

### 11.8 Presenting in Person vs. Remote

#### 11.8.1 In-Person Presentations: The Traditional Approach

Advantages: immediate feedback, better engagement, networking. Challenges: logistics, limited reach, time-intensive. Best practices: arrive early, engage informally in breaks, provide handouts.

#### 11.8.2 Remote Presentations: The Digital Frontier

Advantages: global reach, flexibility (recordings), cost-effectiveness. Challenges: technical glitches, distracted audience, building rapport. Best practices: test tech tools, foster interaction, post-presentation engagement.

#### 11.8.3 Key Takeaway In-Person & Remote Presentation

Side-by-side summary of the above; remote adds tech check, engagement tools (polls, chat, Q&A) and professional or neutral virtual backgrounds.

#### 11.8.4 Hybrid Presentations: Best of Both Worlds

Main challenge: consistent experience for both audiences. Use multiple cameras, clear audio (lapel and ambient mics, dedicated feed) and a **moderator** who brings remote questions in live.

#### 11.8.5 Preparing for the Unexpected

Adaptability (acknowledge the glitch, switch mode, reschedule if needed, read the room) and backup plans (offline presentation, phone line, recorded demo, printed handouts, spare equipment).

### 11.9 How to Present Remote

Recommended book: *Look Me in the Eye* (Julie Hansen). The camera is the client's eyes: looking at the screen reads as looking away.
- Engagement: eye contact via the webcam, active listening, verbal affirmations, body language, visual aids.
- Understanding virtual dynamics: lost non-verbal cues, distractions, time zones, technology as risk and tool.
- Setting up: stable internet, HD webcam, noise-cancelling mic; quiet, tidy background; good front lighting; master the platform.
- Content: brevity, visual storytelling, interactivity (polls, quizzes).
- Engaging: strong start (surprising fact, anecdote, rhetorical question), frequent check-ins, **use names**.
- Q&A: dedicated Q&A slots, a moderator for larger groups, acknowledge when research is needed.
- Virtual presence: posture (stand if you can), measured gestures, voice modulation, no distractions.

### 11.10 The Top 20 Tips for Presenting Remote

Condensed: the camera is the client's eyes ("if the camera does not see it, it does not exist"); eye contact with the camera; open-palm gestures; centred in frame; slow movements; solid-coloured background; dress professionally with contrast; **no black, no stripes or checks** (moiré); speak to one person, not a group; notes near the camera; memorise value propositions, metrics and benefits; define roles and a **back channel** with your producer or Sales partner; tech rehearsal; join early; admit participants, watch chat; record or take notes; offline debrief; pick **one** improvement area at a time; mic about four finger-widths from the mouth; no backlight; update webcam software; **40–90 cm (1–2.5 ft)** from the camera; script as guide, not crutch.

### 11.11 The Top 20 for Presenting in Person

Genuine rapport (eye contact, smile); listen actively to reactions; body language and purposeful movement; simple slides; audience participation; breakout discussions; timed breaks; networking breaks; manage distractions gracefully; vocal dynamics and pauses; personalise; practise relentlessly; receive feedback gracefully; stay flexible. Plus: professional attire, storytelling, judicious props, visible visual aids, backup equipment, gauge mood beforehand, admit unknowns, stay hydrated, humour wisely, concise summary at the end.

### 11.12 Key Takeaways

**Before the demo:** know the client's **top 3–5 interests** (prepare and show top-down); proper discovery (**the first discovery should not exceed 30 minutes; after 25 minutes offer a second discovery** to test seriousness); understand the audience and personas; plan and rehearse; test the software; gather resources (testimonials, case studies, competitive info); prepare for questions; **send a mail-out** with meeting details, agenda, recording and webcam expectations; set objectives; tech check; backup plans; research the company; competitor analysis; stick to the agenda.

**During the demo:** **Tell-Show-Tell**; start with the most important; real-world examples; storytelling; comparison points; visual aids; demonstrate ROI; do not overload; encourage participation; stay flexible; interactive elements; benefits over features; stay positive under objections; answer technical questions; **role-play the named persona** ("I am Kevin, I do…", not "you can…"); pause to ask questions; **breaks at least every 45 minutes** (use a break timer); ask people to switch on webcams; **at least 10 minutes at the end for questions, a mutual action plan and next steps**.

**After the demo:** feedback mechanisms; documentation; engage non-participants; record key insights; referral request; document next steps in writing; nurture the relationship; thank-you note; **the responsible Sales person calls every participant one by one**; share recording and material via a content-sharing tool (e.g. Paperflite); answer open questions; organise the follow-up meeting.

## 13. Proof of Concept (PoC) Execution

A PoC is the bridge between a doubtful client and your promise: tailored proof that the product meets their specific requirements.

**PoC preparation:** understand the client's needs; set clear, **measurable objectives**; tailor the demonstration to the client's workflows, data and scenarios; engage the right resources (product specialists, solution architects, product managers).

**PoC execution:** open with a narrative; show benefits, not every feature (less is more); encourage interaction and scepticism; address concerns promptly; conclude by summarising value and gathering feedback.

**Measuring PoC success:** feedback forms, follow-up discussions with key stakeholders, decision timeframes (a quick decision after the PoC indicates effectiveness).

### 13.1 Why Is the PoC Crucial in PreSales and How to Avoid It?

- **Why crucial:** reduces perceived risk; tangible proof; catalyses the decision.
- **When to avoid it:** needs can be proven through existing **case studies, testimonials or reference calls**; requirements too intricate or niche to reflect fairly (risk of disappointing high expectations); **time**: PoCs need preparation, execution and follow-up, and a tight cycle or competing priorities may not allow it.

### 13.2 Setting the Stage: Initial Steps

Four steps: **identify objectives** (validate a feature, test integration, show scalability); **set scope and boundaries** (convincing but manageable); **allocate resources** (dedicated team, tools, access, permissions); **establish the timeline** (defined start and end date).

### 13.3 Collaborative Planning

**Stakeholder identification** (decision makers, business heads, end users; involvement turns them into champions); **requirement gathering** with the prospect; **success criteria** jointly defined (performance metrics to user satisfaction). Without them a PoC is "sailing without a destination".

### 13.4 Technical Execution

**Environment setup** mirroring the client's infrastructure; **data handling** (robust protection for client data, or realistic dummy data); **iterative development** (implement, test, gather feedback); **documentation** of configurations, customisations and results.

### 13.5 Regular Check-ins

**Progress updates** (progress, challenges, findings); **feedback loops**; **adaptive modifications**.

### 13.6 PoC Evaluation

**Measure against success criteria**; **quantitative analysis** (performance, speed, efficiency gains); **qualitative analysis** (experience, ease of use, perceived value); **cost-benefit analysis** (incl. intangibles).

### 13.7 Presentation of Findings

**Structured reporting** (initial challenges, objectives, scope, evolution, findings, recommendations); **visual aids**; **testimonials** from PoC participants; **next steps** (refine, pilot phase, or full-scale implementation).

### 13.8 Post-PoC Activities

**Feedback collection** to refine future PoCs; **relationship nurturing** even without an immediate deal; **knowledge sharing** across the organisation.

## 14. RFX Process (RFI/RFP/RFQ)

**Definitions:**
- **RFI (Request for Information)** — gathers information to understand the market and define requirements. A chance for a first impression and a relationship.
- **RFP (Request for Proposal)** — requirements are complex, the solution is not defined, a solution to a problem is sought; persuasive, tailored proposal.
- **RFQ (Request for Quotation)** — the buyer knows exactly what they want and needs the cost, typically for standardised items.

An RFP that arrives unannounced means you missed the first act: competitors may already have shaped it. "Qualify as hard as possible to ensure that you have a chance and do not waste your time."

### 14.1 The Unspoken Truth: Timing Matters

An RFP written around a competitor's strengths is a sign someone got there first. Early engagement gives insight into the client's mindset, shapes perceptions (trusted advisor) and can legitimately influence the RFP through genuine expertise. Achieve it through relationships, proactivity (thought leadership) and understanding the client's ecosystem.

### 14.2 Qualify the RFX as Hard as You Can, and Do Not Waste Your Time

Qualification criteria:
1. **Issuer's objectives** — genuine need, or a procedural exercise (price benchmarking, validating a decision already made for another vendor)?
2. **Technical and commercial fit** — does it play to your strengths? Is it cost-driven while you are premium-priced?
3. **Competitive landscape** — tailored to a competitor's offering? Where do you clearly outshine?
4. **Time and resources** — effort (customisation, PoCs, integrations) vs. potential reward.
5. **Long-term strategic value** — a foot in the door or a desired relationship can justify the investment.

Time is the one resource you cannot manufacture: engage selectively.

### 14.3 Navigating the RFX Landscape

Early engagement (be a trusted advisor, not a vendor in the wings); know your strengths and weaknesses (partnerships or workarounds for weak areas); collaboration (Sales, PreSales, technical teams, post-sales or client success, aligned on one value proposition); attention to detail (answer every part; gaps read as lack of interest).

### 14.4 Crafting Compelling Responses

Answer the **"why"**, not just the "what"; use-case scenarios in the client's industry; **quantify benefits** (savings, efficiency, ROI); client testimonials and references from similar clients; clear and concise, free of jargon, for readers from technical experts to executives.

### 14.5 Beyond the Submission

**Follow up a few days after submission** (confirm receipt, offer clarification); prepare for shortlist presentations and demos (anticipate probing questions, align the team); if not selected, **seek feedback** (price, gaps, understanding, external factors).

### 14.6 Usage of Tools for RFP Responses

RFP tools provide centralised, growing **content libraries**; AI-suggested answers combining multiple content areas; real-time collaboration (editing, comments, tasks, deadlines); integration with CRM (e.g. Salesforce), Slack and content platforms (e.g. Paperflite); analytics on what wins. Tool lists are on www.presales-handbook.com.

## 15. Objection Handling

Handle objections like a diplomat: listen, stay calm, understand the underlying concern, find a solution, and bring the conversation back to resolution. Objections are chances to improve the pitch and learn about the client.

**Four types of objections (V78):**
- **Latent** — hidden at first; surface later; tied to underlying or unspoken needs.
- **Expressed** — voiced directly (capabilities, price, implementation).
- **Valid** — real gaps in the solution or genuine concerns about fit.
- **Smoke screen** — distractions or minor concerns that mask deeper, more critical objections.

**Five-step framework:**
1. **Listen actively** — fully understand before responding.
2. **Empathise** — recognise validity without agreeing ("I understand where you are coming from").
3. **Ask probing questions** — find the root (for "too complex": the UI, integration, or unnecessary features?).
4. **Address the objection** — direct and honest; acknowledge known limitations, then workarounds or roadmap.
5. **Confirm resolution** — check the concern is resolved.

**Common technical objections:**
- "Does not integrate with our systems" → current integrations; custom or middleware options; integration roadmap.
- "Performance/scalability issues" → similar-scale references; recent upgrades; offer a performance test or PoC.
- "Security features seem lacking" → security measures, certifications, compliance; third-party audits; upcoming enhancements.

**Common non-technical objections:**
- "The price is too high" → value proposition and ROI; discounts or bundles; phased implementation.
- "We already work with another vendor" → pains with the current vendor; your unique advantages; side-by-side comparison or pilot.
- "Long, complex implementation" → detailed timeline with milestones; post-sale support and training; smooth-implementation references.

**Persistent objections:** propose a pilot or trial; engage subject matter experts; request a follow-up after internal discussion.

**Objection-handling mindset:** stay updated (product, market, competitors); practise active listening; role-play with colleagues.

## 16. Closing

Closing is more than a signature: it is the whole experience from first meeting to agreement, combining science (product, integration, value mechanics) and art (empathy, rapport, storytelling).

**Do not go back to "Demo" or "Discovery" in the closing stage.** It looks unsure, dilutes the narrative from value back to features, and breaks momentum (time gaps invite competitors).

**Path to a seamless close:** consolidate and reiterate value; anticipate last-minute hesitations; foster a spirit of partnership; seek feedback after the close.

### 16.1 Understanding the Role of PreSales in Closing

PreSales now shapes the close through **technical validation** (resolving lingering doubts on integration, scalability, adaptability and translating them into benefits) and **reassuring stakeholders** across committee-style decisions (IT on integration, business leads on future growth).

### 16.2 Setting the Stage for Closing

Closing is the culmination of all prior interactions: **revisit past interactions** (earlier concerns, e.g. scalability, answered as a narrative tied to their plans) and **clarify the proposition** (product, features, customisations, pricing, terms). Re-check alignment if requirements, product or decision makers have changed.

### 16.3 Key Closing Techniques in PreSales

The four V78 closes:
- **The Assumptive Close** — based on positive signals, act as if the sale is made: move the conversation from "if" to "when" and "how".
- **The Summary Close** — recap the journey from the client's challenges to how the product addresses them, cementing the value proposition.
- **The Question Close** — invite remaining concerns: "Is there any reason you would not proceed with our solution?" This surfaces hidden objections.
- **The Incentive Close** — a time-sensitive incentive or bonus to prompt a decision when the process drags; it must be genuine value, not a gimmick.

### 16.4 Nurturing a Relationship Beyond the Close

**Smooth transition to post-sales** (hand over to implementation or client success, remain the technical point of contact); **solicit feedback** through regular, genuine check-ins.

## 17. Close Won / Close Lost Lessons Learned

Every win and loss is a learning source that shapes future success.

### 17.1 Benefits of the Lessons Learned

Fifteen benefits: performance improvement; better product/service understanding; enhanced client insight; improved forecasting; competitive analysis (especially from losses); team morale and growth (no blame); refined marketing strategies; stakeholder communication; budget allocation; stronger client relationships (asking why they decided shows you value them); product development and innovation; targeted training; pricing strategy; risk management (loss patterns as early signals); a continuous **feedback-loop culture**.

### 17.2 How to Execute a Lesson Learned

1. **The opening phase: setting the stage** — set clear goals (what works, what to improve, or both); choose participants from all involved departments and levels; appoint a **neutral facilitator**; circulate a concise project brief (objectives, challenges, outcomes); set ground rules (constructive, **not a blame game**, open and respectful).
2. **The core session: delving deep** — start with successes; then discuss challenges and what could have been done differently; **prioritise** feedback by significance or recurrence; **document** everything (flip chart, whiteboard or digital tool).
3. **Post-session: constructing the future** — categorise feedback (communication, technology, resources); spot patterns and systemic issues; turn each significant insight into an **action item with owner and deadline**; share findings with all stakeholders; **institutionalise** the lessons in processes.
4. **The review: coming full circle** — after some time, check how well the action plans work and adjust.

**Pro tips:** run sessions at significant milestones, not only at the end; create a safe space; use an **external facilitator** for large projects or sensitive topics.

## 18. Staying Updated

### 18.1 The Importance of Staying Updated

Client expectations (clients arrive informed and expect experts); navigating competition; effective communication; rapid technological change; building trust; personal growth; predicting trends; compliance in regulated industries; higher conversions; avoiding misinformation; better problem solving.

### 18.2 Methods to Stay Abreast of Changes

Internal resources (close ties to the product team, internal training, mock demos); industry events and webinars; online courses and certifications (Udemy, Coursera, LinkedIn Learning; vendor certifications); online communities (vendor forums, Reddit, Quora); weekly reading (SaaStr, TechCrunch, Gartner; influencer newsletters); networking and mentors; client feedback; internal tools (CRM news, custom dashboards).

### 18.3 Making It a Habit

Dedicated weekly or bi-weekly learning slots; monthly or quarterly learning goals; accountability partner; reflection and application; alerts and feeds (Google Alerts, Feedly, LinkedIn); study groups; progress log; seek feedback; stay curious; diversify sources; reminders and scheduling.

### 18.4 Organisational Support

**Time allocation:** as a rule of thumb, **no more than 70% of time on deals**. Also: budget for courses, certifications and conferences; internal knowledge-sharing platforms; mentorship programmes; feedback on training; in-house workshops; an LMS; cross-departmental collaboration; recognise and reward learning; industry publication subscriptions; flexible schedules; dedicated learning spaces; continual evaluation of support.

## 19. Documenting and Knowledge Sharing

### 19.1 The Essence of Documenting and Knowledge Sharing

Four benefits: standardisation, efficiency, training (faster onboarding), client trust (quality collateral signals quality service).

### 19.2 Creating Impactful Collateral for Clients

Understand the audience (**demographic** data on people, **firmographic** data on companies); address pain points explicitly; clarity and brevity (no jargon; bullets, infographics, flowcharts); consistent branding; testimonials and case studies **with permission**; regular updates and iterations with client and Sales feedback.

### 19.3 Building an Internal Knowledge Base

Central repository (e.g. Confluence, SharePoint), accessible to all; categorisation (themes, product lines, sales-cycle stages; tags, folders, intuitive hierarchy); standardised templates (client Q&A, product specs, objection handling); version control (archive old versions, mark the latest); feedback loop with **one or two dedicated owners**; accessibility and permissions for sensitive content.

### 19.4 Cultivating a Culture of Knowledge-Sharing

Regular weekly or monthly knowledge-sharing sessions; recognition and rewards; cross-functional collaboration (Sales, post-sales, product); training on the "why" and "how" of documentation.

## 20. Competition

The most overlooked competitor is the **decision to do nothing**.

### 20.1 The Silent Competitor: Doing Nothing

**Nine causes of inertia (status quo), each with countermeasures:**

| Cause | How to win |
|---|---|
| 1. **Fear of change** | Assured onboarding and training; show ease of use |
| 2. **Financial constraints** | Flexible payment plans (tiers, monthly vs. annual, early-bird); ROI demonstrations |
| 3. **Decision paralysis** | Simplified choices and comparison charts; personalised consultations |
| 4. **Organisational culture** | Change management workshops; success stories from similar organisations |
| 5. **Lack of awareness** | Educational marketing (webinars, content, free trials); industry events |
| 6. **Previous negative experiences** | Transparency on capabilities; guarantees or hassle-free refunds |
| 7. **Long-term commitments** | Interoperability with existing tools; migration assistance |
| 8. **Stakeholder buy-in** | Present to decision teams, tailored per stakeholder; comprehensive documentation (security to ROI) |
| 9. **Integration issues** | Robust APIs and integration options; hands-on integration support |

Across all nine: **emphasise the cost of inaction**. Help the client recognise current inefficiencies (pain point identification) and quantify the opportunity cost of doing nothing.

### 20.2 Positive Positioning: The High Road to Differentiation

Do not speak negatively about competitors. It hurts professionalism and credibility, can backfire on market perception, and wastes the chance to articulate your own value. Instead: identify your strengths; articulate benefits, not features; use case studies and testimonials; engage, do not confront (educational content); thought leadership; community building; transparency (roadmap, strengths and limits); communicate cultural and ethical values.

### 20.3 Analysing the Competitive Market for SaaS: A Comprehensive Guide

Six steps:
1. **Define your market** — target audience, geography, product scope; **direct** competitors (near-identical offerings) and **indirect** competitors (other ways to solve the same problem).
2. **Gather information** — competitor websites; analyst reports (V78 names Magic Quadrants and IDC MarketScapes); client reviews (G2, Capterra, TrustRadius); social listening; SEO and ads tools (SEMrush, Ahrefs); trials and demos.
3. **Evaluate strengths and weaknesses** — features, pricing strategy, market position and brand, support and service, financial health.
4. **Identify market gaps** — unserved needs, overlooked segments, service and support gaps.
5. **SWOT analysis** — strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats.
6. **Continuous monitoring** — update the analysis **at least biannually**; stay informed; user feedback loop.

## 21. Personal Branding for the PreSales Professional

Personal branding means marketing yourself and your career as a brand: what people think of when they hear your name. In a trust-driven market it is a snapshot of the value you offer.

**Online and offline brand:** share expertise online (LinkedIn posts, blogs, seminar takeaways, success stories) to become a thought leader. Offline, how you present, engage and listen in meetings and events matters as much; show willingness to learn and collaborate.

**Networking:** meaningful relationships, not business-card collecting. It brings trend insight, diverse perspectives and support with new tools or methods, and lets you help others.

**Community involvement:** participate in forums, webinars and events, and give back (mentoring, sharing resources, volunteering).

## 22. PreSales Leadership

### 22.1 The Anatomy of PreSales Leadership

Seven traits: visionary thinking; team development (personal growth paths); collaboration champion; continuous-learning advocate; stakeholder communication; resolving issues with other departments (conflict-resolution strategy, liaison roles, standard protocols); creating and executing training (needs analysis, tailored programmes, regular sessions, effectiveness metrics).

### 22.2 Challenges Faced by PreSales Leaders

Resource allocation; skill-gap identification; maintaining consistency across demos and pitches; inter-departmental synergy (Sales targets vs. product feasibility); an evolving client landscape.

### 22.3 Strategies for Effective PreSales Leadership

Ten: weekly or bi-weekly team sync-ups; training and development; feedback mechanisms; **data-driven decisions with metrics and KPIs** (ch. 23); regular stakeholder communication; leaders stepping into client interactions; time management (calendar blocking); mentorship; scenario planning; fostering innovation.

### 22.4 Stabilize the Team

- Why: productivity, morale, consistent client experience; turnover has direct and hidden costs.
- Threats: fast-moving technology, high turnover, frequent strategy changes, misalignment with other departments, weak sales performance.
- Levers: continuous training; open communication and **clear career paths**; inter-departmental collaboration; mental health and well-being (flexible hours, wellness, counselling); celebrate achievements; feedback mechanisms; **premium hardware** (laptops, webcams, phones).
- Author's tip: a team **survey every six months** on performance, gaps, leadership strengths and weaknesses, then joint action plans.

### 22.5 Develop a PreSales Playbook

Why: consistent handling of diverse client scenarios. Contents: the **PreSales vision** (the playbook's North Star); detailed processes (outreach to feedback, actionable for juniors); best practices and real scenarios (incl. objection responses); tools and resources; continuous learning; feedback and iteration; room for customisation and personal touch.

### 22.6 Cultivating the Next Generation of PreSales Leaders

Mentoring; leadership workshops; cross-functional projects; ownership and responsibility; networking; soft-skill development (communication, negotiation, empathy).

### 22.7 Meetings & Workshops

**Quarterly** PreSales team meetings (with guest speakers); meet the Sales organisation **at least semi-annually** (regional or global); appreciation (memorable rewards, e.g. a wellness weekend or a watch, as lasting reminders of the win); strategy sessions beyond the annual Sales Kick-off; workshops with Product Management, Development and Marketing (incl. a client-feedback day followed by an internal roadmap day).

### 22.8 Survey

- **PreSales survey:** resource adequacy (people, tools, training); collaboration with Sales; product feedback.
- **Sales survey:** PreSales support quality; training needs; collateral requirements.
- **Craft well:** clear questions, include open-ended questions, anonymity, act on the feedback and share the findings.

### 22.9 Balancing the Team / Team Sizing

**Roles (the pillars):** Solution Consultant (SC) / Sales Engineer (SE); Solution Consultant Manager or Senior SC; Demo Automation Expert; Events and Trade Shows Specialist; Demo Enablement; RFP Manager; PoC Supporter; PoC Coordinator; IT & Security Specialist; Enterprise Solution Principal / Value Engineer; Content & Knowledge Manager.

**Balance:** startups and niche products lean on technical specialists; scaling adds demo automation and events; broad suites need an even spread. Reassess on new products, markets or strategy. Cross-skill the team. **Sizing** depends on scale, product complexity, client diversity and competition: large teams bring depth but coordination cost; small teams are agile but strain at peaks.

### 22.10 Onboarding and Training New PreSales Professionals

Integrate into company DNA and meet cross-functional teams; product proficiency (pair with experienced PreSales, shadow demos); role-playing and simulations; continuous learning pathways (certifications); feedback and mentors; real-world exposure (smaller clients, shadowing); soft skills; regular check-ins, especially in the **first year**.

### 22.11 RACI Matrix

- **Responsible** — the doer (at least one per task).
- **Accountable** — ultimate ownership; **a single point** per task.
- **Consulted** — subject matter experts asked before decisions (e.g. product development on customisation).
- **Informed** — kept in the loop (e.g. Sales on demo outcomes or promised customisations).

Build it by listing all PreSales tasks, listing stakeholders, and assigning R, A, C and I per task. Benefits: clear roles, efficient resources, better communication, fewer overlaps and gaps, ownership. Challenges: overcomplication and ambiguity. Remedies: keep it simple, review and refine, communicate openly.

### 22.12 First 30-60-90 Days as a Leader

- **First 30 days, observing and understanding:** meet each team member individually; understand the workflow and the collaboration with Sales; meet the leaders of other departments. *Listen more than you speak.*
- **Next 60 days, analysis and strategy formulation:** identify gaps; set clear, measurable objectives; share observations in team meetings. *Understand why systems exist before changing them.*
- **Final 90 days, implementation and feedback:** pilot programmes; feedback mechanism; build relationships; review and adjust at day 90. *Leadership is dynamic.*

## 23. Metrics and KPIs for PreSales

**Why metrics matter:** continuous improvement; alignment with organisational goals; resource allocation (highest-ROI activities); informed, data-driven decisions; predictive analysis; client understanding; accountability and performance assessment; identifying bottlenecks; motivation and morale; stakeholder communication.

**Core metrics (V78):**

| Metric | Definition and reading |
|---|---|
| **Demo-to-Close Rate** | Ratio of demos conducted to deals closed; efficacy of the demo |
| **Time Spent on Demos** | Average demo duration; too long = inefficiency, too short = critical points missed |
| **Number of Demos Conducted** | High = strong interest, or qualification too lenient |
| **Lead Response Time** | Speed of PreSales response to new leads; linked to conversion |
| **Client Feedback Scores** | Post-demo surveys on demo quality |
| **Value of the Sales Pipeline** | Total potential revenue of ongoing PreSales opportunities; guides resource allocation |
| **PreSales Team Utilisation** | % of time on core activities vs. admin and non-core tasks |
| **PreSales Touchpoints** | Interactions needed before a lead converts or drops |
| **Follow-up Effectiveness** | Rate at which follow-ups move the lead further down the funnel |

**Advanced KPIs:** customisation requests (high = demos too generic or product gaps); technical issue count during demos; frequency of follow-up questions (clarity of the demo); **competitive win rate** against specific competitors; rate of objection-handling success.

**Client-centric metrics:** Net Promoter Score after the demo (likelihood to recommend); client satisfaction right after an interaction.

**Collaboration and internal metrics:** Sales-PreSales alignment (frequency of meetings and information sharing); training and development hours; tool and software utilisation rates (tool ROI).

**Efficiency metrics:** cost per demo (tools, personnel time, other expenses); demos per PreSales rep (workload distribution); time to prepare a demo.

**Leveraging data for continuous improvement:** regular metric reviews; client feedback loop; **benchmarking** against industry standards and competitors.
