# ABC: Individual Behaviour Change

> **Credit.** Study note in our own words. The Theory of Constraints thinking processes originate with Eliyahu M. Goldratt. The Antecedent–Behaviour–Consequence model has its roots in behavioural psychology. This tool, as used here, follows the Black Belt in Thinking (BBiT) programme ([blackbeltinthinking.com](https://blackbeltinthinking.com/)). The method remains the work of its authors.

## What it is

People repeat behaviours that pay off *for them*, as they perceive it, and drop those that cost them. ABC breaks this into three parts:
- **Antecedent:** the trigger that creates the chance to act. It does not force the behaviour.
- **Behaviour:** the action itself, not its result.
- **Consequence:** what the person experiences as a result. This drives what they do next time.

Most injections need someone to behave differently. ABC is how you make that happen.

## When to use it in PreSales

- A champion agrees to share the business case internally but never does.
- An AE keeps booking demos before discovery.
- You want a customer team to actually log in during a trial.

## Steps

- [ ] **Analyse:** describe one behaviour. Note its triggers. List positive and negative consequences *for that person*. Rate each 1–5 for impact. Do not add up the scores; look for the strongest few.
- [ ] Ask whether the behaviour should change at all. It may be sensible, or a symptom of a deeper cause.
- [ ] **Adjust:** set the size of change you want. Then add or remove a trigger, change a consequence, or both. For each new consequence, write how it will happen and rate its likely impact on this person.
- [ ] **Replace** if you remove a behaviour: something will fill the gap. Plan the replacement and analyse it separately.
- [ ] **Ethics check:** would you be comfortable telling the person afterwards what you did and why?

## B2B example

Behaviour: the champion does not forward the business case.
Consequences for them: risk of looking pushy to their VP (negative, 4); extra work reformatting it (negative, 2); little visible upside (positive, 1).
Adjustments: provide a version in their company template (removes the rework), and offer a joint slot where the SC presents the numbers (removes the personal risk).

## Common errors

- Judging consequences in general instead of for this person.
- Assuming a reward works for everyone. Public praise can punish someone who dislikes attention.
- Treating change as all or nothing. A partial shift is still progress.
- Rewarding outcomes the person does not control instead of behaviours they do.
