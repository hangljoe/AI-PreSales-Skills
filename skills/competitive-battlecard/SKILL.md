# Competitive Battlecard

Fast, honest competitive positioning for live deals.
Know where we genuinely win, where they're strong, and how to handle their attacks.
Grounded in *The PreSales Handbook* ch. 20 (Competition): the most common competitor is doing nothing (20.1),
you win on your own value rather than on the competitor's flaws (20.2), and a SWOT pulls the analysis together (20.3).

No connections? Name your product, the competitor, and the scope — the skill builds the card from there. It can also use public information you paste (competitor website, analyst notes, review-site comments).

## Step 1 — Tell the skill what you're working with

1. **Competitor** — who are we up against? A named vendor, **the status quo / doing nothing**, or **an in-house build**?
   If it's the status quo or an in-house build, use the status-quo card below instead of Steps 2–4.
2. **Your product and the modules in scope** — what are we selling in this deal?
3. **Your proven differentiators** — capabilities, references, and metrics you can back up (or read them from the deal folder, e.g. `08_competitive-read.md`)
4. **What has the customer said?** — any comments about the competitor, demo requests, pricing mentions
5. **Deal stage** — already in a competitive evaluation, or just preparing?
6. **Primary evaluation contact** — their role (IT, business owner, procurement, C-suite)

If a knowledge base is connected: pull the latest battlecard for this competitor.
If a CRM is connected: check for prior wins and losses against this competitor in similar accounts.
If you don't know the competitor's product well, say so and tag those claims 🔴 Unknown — never invent their weaknesses.

## Status-quo card — "do nothing" or "build it ourselves" (handbook 20.1)

When the real alternative is inertia or an internal build, there is no competitor product to compare.
Build this shorter card instead of Steps 2–4, then continue with the SWOT (Step 5) and the rest.

```
STATUS-QUO CARD — [Account] | Alternative: Do nothing / Build in-house

What the status quo genuinely offers (be honest):
[Familiar workflows, no new spend, no disruption, full control for an in-house build]

Cost of inaction (quantify where possible):
[Time, money or risk lost each month the current process stays. Tag 🟢/🟡/🔴]

Likely causes of the inertia (hypotheses — confirm in discovery):
[e.g. fear of change, budget timing, decision paralysis, a past bad experience,
 an existing contract, stakeholder misalignment, integration worries]

If they plan to build in-house — the true cost of the build:
[Build effort, ongoing maintenance, time to value, opportunity cost of the team,
 key-person risk. Ask; don't assume they can't do it]

Proof moment: [The one demo, reference or number that makes the cost of waiting visible]
```

For a full diagnosis of each inertia cause with counter-strategies, hand off to the `do-nothing-buster` skill.

## Step 2 — Where we genuinely win

List 3–5 defensible, provable strengths versus this competitor for the products in scope.

Rules:
- Each strength needs proof — a demo capability, a customer reference, or a real metric
- Do NOT list things the competitor also has ("we have AI too")
- Focus on *depth*, *integration quality*, and *measurable outcome* — not checkbox features
- Be honest: overclaiming here destroys credibility when the customer does their own research
- Confidence-tag every claim

```
WHERE WE WIN — [Your product] vs [Competitor] — [Scope]

1. [Strength]
   Why it matters: [One sentence on the business outcome this delivers]
   Proof: [Reference customer / demo capability / measurable result]
   Confidence: 🟢 Confirmed / 🟡 Inferred

2. [Strength]
   Why it matters: [...]
   Proof: [...]
   Confidence: 🟢 / 🟡

3. [Strength]
   Why it matters: [...]
   Proof: [...]
   Confidence: 🟢 / 🟡
```

## Step 3 — Where they compete (their strongest claims)

What will the competitor say they're good at? Name their real strengths honestly.
If you pretend they're weak when they're not, you lose credibility when the customer finds out.

```
THEIR STRONGEST CLAIMS

1. [Their genuine strength or claim]
   How to respond: [Our counter — factual, not defensive, not dismissive]
   What to ask next: [A question that shifts the ground without attacking directly]

2. [Their genuine strength or claim]
   How to respond: [...]
   What to ask next: [...]

3. [Their genuine strength or claim]
   How to respond: [...]
   What to ask next: [...]
```

## Step 4 — How to handle their attacks on us

What will they say about your company and product in this competitive situation?

```
THEIR LIKELY ATTACKS ON US

Attack: [What they'll say about us — be realistic]
Response: [How to address it — factual, calm, no defensiveness]
Reframe: [The question or statement that shifts back to what matters]
```

## Step 5 — SWOT (handbook 20.3)

Pull Steps 2–4 (or the status-quo card) into one view for this deal. Strengths and weaknesses are
*ours relative to this competitor*; opportunities and threats come from the customer's situation and the market.

| Strengths (ours, vs them) | Weaknesses (ours, vs them) |
|---------------------------|----------------------------|
| [From Step 2, with proof] | [Honest gaps — where they are stronger] |
| **Opportunities** | **Threats** |
| [Unmet needs, gaps in their offer or support, customer events we can use] | [Their incumbency, price pressure, a stakeholder who prefers them, inertia] |

Tag each entry 🟢/🟡/🔴. A SWOT with no weaknesses is not honest; add at least one.

## Step 6 — Discovery questions when a competitor is in play

Use these to understand where the competitor stands and find gaps in their solution —
without ever directly attacking them.

```
COMPETITIVE DISCOVERY QUESTIONS

"What did [Competitor] show you that you found most compelling?"
→ Learn what the customer values most — and where to go deeper

"Is there anything they showed you that raised questions or felt unclear?"
→ Surface gaps without attacking directly

"What are the top three criteria you're using to make this decision?"
→ Find out if the evaluation criteria favour us or them — and if not, change them

"Who on your side is driving the evaluation — and are all the stakeholders aligned?"
→ Identify internal champions for each vendor

"Have you spoken to any of their reference customers?"
→ Test how deep their validation has gone

"What would a failed implementation look like for you — what's the risk you're managing?"
→ Gets at the deeper criteria beyond features and price
```

## Step 7 — Deal strategy recommendation

Based on the above, here is the recommended approach:

```
COMPETITIVE DEAL STRATEGY

Competitor: [Name] | Stage: [Where we are in the deal]
Our position: Ahead / Behind / Even [be honest]

Priority action 1: [The single highest-leverage thing to do right now]
Priority action 2: [Second priority]
Priority action 3: [Third priority]

Proof moment:
[The demo moment, reference or metric where our strength is visible on its own merits —
 the customer sees the value; we never have to name the competitor's gap]

Their strength, addressed early:
[Their biggest strength — acknowledge it proactively and show how we meet the same need,
 rather than waiting for the customer to raise it]
```

Positive positioning (handbook 20.2): talk about our value, not their flaws. Never criticise the competitor
to the customer. It costs credibility and says nothing about what we bring.

## Quality checklist

- [ ] Strengths are honest — each has a proof point, not wishful thinking
- [ ] Competitor's strengths are acknowledged — no underestimating
- [ ] Attacks and responses are factual, not emotional
- [ ] Discovery questions are open-ended — not leading
- [ ] SWOT has at least one honest weakness and one threat
- [ ] Proof moments show our value on its own merits — no criticism of the competitor
- [ ] Status quo or in-house build considered as the alternative, even when a named competitor is in play
- [ ] Deal strategy has one clear priority action — not a list of ten
- [ ] All claims confidence-tagged (🟢 Confirmed / 🟡 Inferred / 🔴 Unknown)
