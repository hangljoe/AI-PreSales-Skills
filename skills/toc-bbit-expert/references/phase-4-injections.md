## Phase 4 — Break the Assumptions (Injections)

**Goal: Find what to change to. This is TOC Question 2.**

An injection is not a compromise. A compromise means both sides get less than they need. An injection means the conflict becomes unnecessary — because the assumption holding it in place is no longer true.

**The golden rule: an injection is a state, never an action.**
Word it as an outcome that must exist ("the pilot environment has live ERP data"), not a task ("ask the customer's IT team for an extract"). The state is your target. The first action you try might not work, and different people may need different actions to reach the same state. Naming the state keeps everyone aiming at the same goal — and keeps options open.

### The two-step injection process

**Step 1 — Raise ALL assumptions. Do not filter.**

For every connection you want to break, list every assumption behind it:
- Cause-and-effect: "If A, then B, *because*..."
- Prerequisite: "In order to A, I must B, *because*..."

Write down every assumption, including the ones that seem obvious or wrong. The first ones will be well-known and often already tried. The breakthrough injection is usually found further down the list — in the creative territory you only reach by pushing past the obvious. Trying to write only the "good" ones usually gets you two assumptions, then you stall.

**Step 2 — Reverse each assumption into a candidate injection.**

Flip each assumption. The reversal of "only IT can approve integrations" doesn't have to be "anyone can" — "a pre-approved integration pattern exists" also breaks it. Pick the wording that is most useful. You can get more than one injection from a single assumption.

Then pick the injection you like best and test it: *"If this were true right now, would it break the connection?"* If yes, it is a valid injection.

Worry about the actions to make it true *later*. Right now, you only need the target state.

### For core conflicts: go deeper

Core conflicts are deeply embedded — they are not held in place by obvious assumptions. The obvious injections have usually been tried before with limited results.

**Aim for 10–30 assumptions on the key connection.** The early ones are well-known. As you push further, you enter more creative territory — that is where the most powerful injections live.

**Chase the injection with the best impact, not the easiest.** Good core conflict injections are often ambitious — they may require a long-term plan, significant investment, or a structural change. Entertain the ambitious ones before writing them off.

### Injection rules (a BBiT expert enforces every one)

1. **An injection must break a named assumption** — not split the difference between WA and WB.
2. **Develop multiple injections** — never stop at the first one.
3. **Test for win-win** — both Need A and Need B must remain fully satisfied. If one side still loses something real, it is a compromise, not an injection.
4. **Test for new UDEs** — does the injection create new problems? If yes, add a preventative injection for each.
5. **Select the simplest effective injection** — the one requiring the fewest resources, the least time, and the most leverage per unit of effort.
6. **For clouds: break your own side first.** Breaking the other person's side makes people defensive. You still get the effect you want, and you avoid a fight.

### Expert questions for injection development

- "If this assumption weren't true, would the conflict disappear?"
- "What would need to change in the *system* — not just in one person's behaviour — for both needs to be met simultaneously?"
- "Is there a way to satisfy Need A through a completely different mechanism than Want A?"
- "What would make this conflict obsolete?"
- "What would a systems designer do here, rather than a negotiator?"
- "What do other systems do when they face this same conflict?"
