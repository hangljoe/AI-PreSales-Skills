## ABC — Individual Behaviour Change

> **Credit.** Study note in our own words. TOC thinking processes originate with Eliyahu M. Goldratt. The Antecedent–Behaviour–Consequence model comes from behavioural psychology. This tool follows the Black Belt in Thinking programme ([blackbeltinthinking.com](https://blackbeltinthinking.com/)).

*Use when you need to understand why one specific person does something, or to design a change in how they act. Invoke standalone, or after the BBiT analysis when an injection depends on someone behaving differently.*

### What it is

People keep doing what pays off for them, as they see it, and drop what costs them. ABC splits this into three parts:

| Part | What it is | Key point |
|------|-----------|-----------|
| **Antecedent** | The trigger that creates the chance to act | It prompts a choice. It does not force one. |
| **Behaviour** | The action itself | Not the result. People control actions, not always results. |
| **Consequence** | What the person experiences afterwards | Only consequences for *this person*, as *they* perceive them, count. |

### When to use it in PreSales

- A champion agrees to take the business case to their CFO but never does.
- An AE keeps booking demos before discovery is done.
- Pilot users say yes in meetings but do not log in.

### Step 1 — Analyse

- [ ] Name one observable behaviour (the action, not the outcome).
- [ ] List its triggers: external (a calendar invite) or internal (habit).
- [ ] List positive and negative consequences for this person. Rate each 1–5 for impact.
- [ ] Do not sum the scores. One negative 5 can outweigh many positive 2s. Look for the strongest few.
- [ ] Ask whether the behaviour should change at all. It may be sensible, or a symptom of a deeper cause. If so, run the other BBiT tools on that cause.

### Step 2 — Adjust

- [ ] Set the magnitude of change first (small shift, big increase, near elimination).
- [ ] **Antecedent route** (often fastest): add a prompt to increase a rare but rewarding behaviour. Remove the trigger to reduce a context-bound one.
- [ ] **Consequence route** (most common): make the wanted behaviour more rewarding and less costly, or the reverse.
- [ ] For each consequence change, write: the new consequence, how it will really happen (a mechanism, not a hoped-for attitude shift), and its likely impact 1–5 for this person.
- [ ] Match impact to magnitude. A distant annual reward rarely moves a daily habit.

### Step 3 — Replace (when removing a behaviour)

- [ ] Something will fill the gap. Plan the replacement behaviour and analyse it separately.
- [ ] Mirror-image behaviours (updating the CRM vs. not updating it) can share one table. Distinct behaviours need their own.
- [ ] **Ethics check:** would you be comfortable telling the person afterwards what you did and why? If not, rethink.

### B2B example

Behaviour: the customer's IT lead skips the weekly pilot check-in.
Antecedent: the invite lands in a slot that clashes with their change board.
Consequences for them: meeting feels like a vendor status update (negative, 4); no decisions are made that affect their team (negative, 3); keeps goodwill with the project sponsor (positive, 2).
Adjustments: move the slot off change-board day (antecedent). Put open integration decisions on the agenda that only the IT lead can make, and send a two-line decision log afterwards (consequence: their time now shapes the outcome).

### Common errors

- Judging consequences in general instead of for this person.
- Assuming one reward suits everyone. Public praise can punish someone who dislikes attention.
- Treating change as all or nothing. A partial shift is progress.
- Rewarding outcomes the person does not control instead of actions they do.
